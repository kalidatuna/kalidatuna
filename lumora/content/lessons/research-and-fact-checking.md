AI can compress hours of research into minutes. But research is also where AI's habit of confident invention is most dangerous. This lesson shows you how to get the speed *and* keep the accuracy.

## Know which mode you're in

| Mode | What it does | Use for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Plain chat** | Answers from training data (may be out of date) | Explanations, brainstorming, general knowledge |
| **Web search** | Searches the web and cites sources | Current facts, prices, news, specific details |
| **Deep research** | Runs many searches, reads dozens of pages, writes a cited report (takes minutes) | Big questions: comparing products, market research, background on a topic |

If you need facts that may have changed recently, make sure web search or research mode is on.

## A great research prompt

```prompt
Research question: [question]. Context: I'm [who you are] and I need this to [decision/purpose]. Please:
- Prioritise official, primary and recent sources (say how recent).
- Separate established facts from opinions and estimates.
- Note where sources disagree.
- End with what's still uncertain and how I could find out.
```

## The 3-check verification habit

AI citations can be wrong. The link might not exist, or the page might not say what the AI claims. For anything that matters:

1. **Click the source.** Does it exist? Is it reputable?
2. **Find the claim.** Does the page actually say that?
3. **Cross-check.** Does a second independent source agree?

> [!TIP]
> Ask: *"Which claims in your answer are you least confident about?"* Assistants are surprisingly good at flagging their own weak spots when asked directly.

## Reading faster with AI

- Paste a long article or upload a report: "Summarise the main argument, the evidence used, and the weakest point."
- Compare two documents: "What are the key differences between these two policies?"
- Get the counter-argument: "What would a thoughtful critic say about this article?"

## Spotting misinformation

AI can help you check claims you see online:

```prompt
I saw this claim online: "[claim]". Help me evaluate it. What would need to be true for it to be accurate? What do reliable sources say? What are common ways this kind of claim is misleading?
```

## Try it now

Pick a purchase or decision you're researching. Run it through a deep-research or web-search mode with the prompt above. Then verify three claims using the 3-check habit.

## Key takeaways

- Use web search or deep research for anything current or factual.
- Ask for primary sources, disagreements and uncertainty.
- Click, find, cross-check: never trust a citation you haven't opened.
